Activities and preparation

Looking and the 2×2 diagonal-tile world

Look closely at four objects, then make 2×2 designs with diagonal tiles. At the next meeting organize all the fixed-orientation designs by the number of each diagonal.

Try this and bring it back
Show why your organization accounts for every design. Invent another situation with two choices at each position.

Pingala and 1–2 tilings

Make all the strips you can from pieces of lengths 1 and 2. Organize them by the last piece.

Try this and bring it back
Explain how shorter strips account for the count for a longer strip.

Change the allowed pieces

Try lengths 1, 2, and 3, then try 2 and 3. Record the first few counts before conjecturing a rule.

Try this and bring it back
Use a last-piece argument to explain which earlier counts matter.

  • Week 2 MLA Notebook
Transformations and symmetry

Track what a horizontal flip, vertical flip, or half-turn does to a design. Try applying two moves in succession.

Try this and bring it back
Bring one example of a move that changes a design and one that leaves a design looking the same.

Frieze patterns

Use a single motif to make repeating strips. Check each strip for reflections, half-turns, and glide reflections.

Try this and bring it back
Make two strips from the same motif with different symmetries. Mark the moves you found.

From a line to the wall

Repeat a motif in two directions on a square grid. Compare what you have to repeat with the smallest piece needed to generate the design.

Try this and bring it back
In Looking Back, Looking Forward, revisit two or three ideas, images, or questions from the first six weeks. What might you explore in an original artwork?

Harder square-grid wallpaper patterns

Explore glide reflections and diagonal symmetries. Try making a p4m pattern and investigate the role of the motif.

Try this and bring it back
Bring one arrangement that nearly works and explain which required symmetry you still need.

Change the grid

Move from the square grid to a triangular or hexagonal grid. Look for threefold and sixfold rotations.

Try this and bring it back
Choose a structure from the course that you would like to develop in your own work.

Build a Mathematical World

Develop an original mathematical work. Test a choice, document a change, and explain the structure you found.

Try this and bring it back
Share one early version and explain a mathematical decision that changed what you made.

Combinatorial games

Play a small subtraction game and record positions that are losing with optimal play. Use smaller positions to explain larger ones.

Try this and bring it back
Change one allowed move and investigate which positions change.

Wythoff, Fibonacci, and the golden ratio

Organize losing positions in Wythoff’s game and look for a ratio. Revisit ratios of successive Fibonacci numbers.

Try this and bring it back
Explain what your ratios appear to approach and what evidence you have.

Tiling with triangles and quadrilaterals

Trace copies of a triangle or quadrilateral and build a patch. Use marked corners or a half-turn to explain why pieces fit.

Try this and bring it back
Bring a photo or sketch of a patch and mark a point where the corners meet.

Regular polygons and pentagonal tilings

Make edge-to-edge patterns first. Then record the polygons around one vertex and use 60°, 90°, 120°, 135°, and 150° to find arrangements summing to 360°. Test regular and selected irregular pentagons.

Try this and bring it back
Sketch two different 360° arrangements. Try growing one into a patch and record what happens.

From compass circles to geometric design

Play with overlapping circles, construct a hexagon and square, and explore a design inspired by the Great Mosque of Herat. Then vary a shared construction.

Try this and bring it back
Start with twelve marks. Use some or all of them, draw arcs, or work with new intersections. Bring your drawing and explain one choice.

One Structure, Many Possibilities

Start with a geometric construction or tiling. Make two meaningful variations and use peer feedback to revise.

Try this and bring it back
Show your process and explain what changed and which mathematical relationships you preserved.
